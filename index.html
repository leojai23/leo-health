What Does Satvic Mean?

Lord Krishna, in the Bhagavad Gita, states that all embodied souls work under the control of three gunas — modes, or qualities, of material nature. The thoughts in our head, the activities we perform, the people we meet and the food we eat can all be classified as Satvic, Rajasik or Tamasik.

Satvic — Mode of Goodness
Purity, happiness, compassion, bliss, love, self-control, satisfaction, non-violence, fearlessness, surrender.
Rajasik — Mode of Passion
Arrogance, ego, restlessness, anxiety, anger, impatience, fear, uncontrollable desires, distress.
Tamasik — Mode of Ignorance
Laziness, tiredness, depression, lethargy, ignorance, apathy, inertia, illusion.

One person can have multiple modes. When Satvic dominates, we feel happy, satisfied and in control of our senses. When Rajasik dominates, we feel restless, anxious and angry. When Tamasik dominates, we feel lazy, tired, depressed and lethargic. Our modern lifestyle, with its high stress and toxins, fluctuates between Rajasik and Tamasik — to achieve happiness we have to transcend to Satvic.

Food in the three modes (Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 17)

Foods in the mode of goodness increase the duration of life, purify one’s existence and give strength, health, happiness and satisfaction. Such foods are juicy, fatty, wholesome, and pleasing to the heart.Verse 8

Satvic food

Foods that are fresh, wholesome (unprocessed, unrefined), juicy (water-rich), freshly cooked and lightly seasoned. It is living food, with life energy inside it — food straight from Nature, with no or minimal human interference.

  • All fresh fruits — melons, oranges, papaya, apple, pear, berries, grapes
  • All vegetables — bottle-gourd, ridge-gourd, bell peppers, carrots, spinach, coriander, all leafy greens
  • Whole fats — coconut, soaked nuts & seeds
  • Whole grains — whole wheat (with chokar), brown rice

Rajasik food

Foods that are too bitter, sour, salty, pungent, dry and hot — foods with excess salt and spices. Examples: excess salt, red chili, garam masala, asafoetida (heeng), vinegars; very hot water and very hot herbal tea.

Tamasik food

Foods that are stale (eaten more than 3 hours after cooking), rotten (meat and fish) and foul (bad-smelling). Tamasik food is dead — when we eat dead food, the same death is transferred to our body as disease. Examples: everything packaged, bottled, tinned or canned; meat, fish & eggs; stimulants such as onion, garlic, tea, coffee, alcohol, cigarettes, betel nut (supaari) and betel leaf.

Principle 1 — Living Food

Living foods come straight from Nature, without cooking or processing — eaten in their pristine, raw state. Take a wheat seed, bury it and water it, and it grows into a sapling. The 3-hour rule

According to the Bhagavad Gita (17.10), food should be eaten within 3 hours of being cooked. After 3 hours it starts to lose its life energy and becomes Tamasik. If something is cooked on fire, eat it within 3 hours, maximum 5. Never store cooked food in the refrigerator to eat over several days.

But why apply the 3-hour rule only to sabzi and chapati? Processed biscuits, chips, candies, snacks and namkeens were often cooked years in advance and stored with synthetic chemicals and preservatives. These may increase the shelf life of the product, but they decrease the shelf life of our body.

Sun-cooked, not uncooked

At least 70% of the daily diet should be raw — fruits, vegetables, salads, smoothies, juices, sprouts. ‘Sun-cooked’ is a better term than ‘raw’: a fruit ripening on the tree has been cooked by Mother Nature under the sun. By cooking it on the stove, we’re re-heating it.

Principle 4 — Water-Rich Food

Food can be classified as water-rich or water-poor.

Water-rich
High water content — melons, berries, apples, grapes, oranges, tomatoes, cucumbers; bottle gourd, ash gourd, celery, all leafy greens. Light, easy to digest, and act like laxatives.
Water-poor
Low water content — all grains (rice, wheat), millets, lentils, beans; starchy vegetables such as potato and yam; all nuts and seeds. Harder to digest and can be constipating unless taken in limited amounts.

To identify which a food is, put it in the juicer. If a lot of juice comes out, it’s water-rich. You can’t juice a chapati.

How much of my diet should be water-rich?

Most people eat the exact opposite ratio — a heavy grain-rich meal 3–4 times a day — and as a result they’re drying up. Food Combining

Foods are natural chemicals. The more ingredients in a meal, the greater the chance of a digestive explosion. Even fresh, wholesome food, if paired incorrectly, can cause indigestion, fermentation, gas, bloating and toxins.

The highway analogy

Three categories of vehicles enter the highway of our digestive tract: scooters (fruits — light and quick, ~3 hours), cars (vegetables — ~6 hours), and trucks (grains, lentils, nuts, seeds — heavy and slow, ~18 hours). This is why we feel lazy and sleepy after too many grains.

Note

Neutral vegetables (lettuce, celery, spinach, coriander, cucumber) digest quicker than starchy vegetables (potato, peas, pumpkin, cauliflower).

Six laws of food combining

How to Grow Sprouts

Sprouts are the very first shoots of germinating seeds, legumes and grains. Nutritionally they have a long history as a health food — 10 to 30 times more nutritious than the best vegetables, because they are baby plants in their prime. Applying water to a seed releases dormant vitamins, minerals and enzymes — most pronounced in the first 12 days of growth. Sprouts are also biogenic (they create new life when planted) and alive.

Leafy sprouts vs lentil / bean sprouts

Leafy sprouts — easy to digest, suitable for everyone
Alfalfa, clover, fenugreek, radish.
Lentil & bean sprouts — difficult to digest
Moong, red lentils, green lentils, chickpea. Suitable only for athletes, children and manual labourers; avoid while curing a disease, losing weight or maintaining health.

Six simple steps

  1. Rinse the seeds and place in a glass container. Cover with filtered water plus an inch. Soak overnight.
  2. Next morning, drain the water. Rinse once and drain again. Put the seeds in the centre of a cotton cloth.
  3. Tie the cloth tightly so the seeds are contained in a bundle.
  4. Place the bundle in a bowl and cover with a plate. Keep out of direct sunlight.
  5. Rinse the seeds with fresh filtered water twice a day — morning and evening — for 4–5 days.
  6. When the sprouts are long enough to eat, let them dry completely before storing. They keep in a covered container in the refrigerator for 5–7 days.

How to Make Nut Milk

Nut milks are made by soaking nuts in water for 6–8 hours, then blending with fresh water and straining. Coconut milk is the healthiest — easier to digest than any other. Most recipes in this book already include coconut milk, so there is no need to drink nut milk separately. Never buy pre-packaged nut milks — they are full of preservatives, thickeners and artificial ingredients.

Method — coconut milk (makes 2 cups)

  1. Take 1 cup of fresh dessicated coconut.
  2. Combine with 2 cups of water in a blender.
  3. Blend until smooth.
  4. Pour the mixture over a bowl covered with a nut milk bag or muslin cloth.
  5. Squeeze out the milk with your hand. Use the leftover pulp as a face scrub.
  6. Use immediately or store in the refrigerator for up to 1–2 days.

For almond milk, replace the 1 cup dessicated coconut with 1 cup almonds soaked in water for 5–6 hours.

Note

Use only raw, un-fried nuts — strictly no roasted nuts. Always discard the soaking water. Do not make soy milk. Never cook coconut milk directly on flame — when cooked it converts into cholesterol; add it towards the end after switching off the stove.

  2. Conversion chart: ¼ cup = 4 tablespoons; ½ cup = 8 tablespoons; 1 tablespoon = 3 teaspoons; 1 pinch = 1/16 teaspoon.
  3. When using dry herbs, use less — a 1:3 ratio. 1 tablespoon fresh = 1 teaspoon dry.
  4. Always soak seeds and nuts before using — soaking removes the enzyme inhibitors that make them hard to digest. Average soak time is 6 hours; overnight is fine.
  5. Stick to the recipes exactly. Do not increase the amount of grains, nuts or spices — the recipes are built on strict Satvic principles.

Breakfast & Smoothie Basics

For breakfast, have fresh fruits, a smoothie or the pure Satvic salad, 2 hours after your morning detox juice. If you have fruits for breakfast, have salad for dinner, and vice versa. If diabetic, avoid sweet fruits (mango, pineapple, chikoo, banana) and stick to neutral fruits (melon, papaya).

Eating fruit

  • 1 fruit at a time (best) — ‘mono-eating’; a plate of any one seasonal fruit (melon, apple, pear, pineapple, orange, papaya, peach, pomegranate, berries, guava).
  • 2 fruits — mix same categories; melons only with melons; don’t mix sweet (mango, chikoo) with citric (orange, pineapple, kiwi).
  • 2 or more fruits — not bad, but slower to digest; still mix only same categories, never citrus with sweet.

The 4 components of a smoothie

  1. Liquid — coconut milk, coconut water, watery fruits or drinking water.
  2. Base — any fruit or vegetable: banana, pear, melon, papaya, beetroot, carrot, spinach, lettuce.
  3. Sweetener — sweet fruits such as mango, banana or dates.
  4. Flavor — herbs such as mint or basil, carob powder, cacao powder, vanilla powder, ginger.

Satvic Khichadi

Serves 3

Brown rice is wholesome but still a grain, so this is a comfort food rather than a healing recipe — still far healthier than traditional khichadi made with ghee, lentils, white rice and spices.

Ingredients

  • ¾ cup soaked brown rice
  • 6 cups water
  • 1 cup finely chopped green beans
  • 1 cup grated carrot
  • 1 cup grated bottle gourd
  • 1 teaspoon turmeric powder
  • 1 cup finely chopped spinach
  • 2 small green chillies, finely crushed
  • 1 cup chopped tomato
  • ½ cup coconut kernel, sliced then blended
  • 2 teaspoons rock salt
  • ½ cup chopped coriander
  • Green Chutney, to serve

Method

  3. Add the spinach and green chillies. Stir and cook 5 minutes.
  4. Turn off the stove. Add the tomatoes, coconut and salt. Keep covered 5 minutes.
  5. Top with coriander and serve with green chutney.

Satvic Daliya

Serves 2–3

Less grain, more vegetables — a 1:3 ratio, so the body spends less time digesting and more time healing.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup broken wheat porridge (daliya)
  • 1½ teaspoons cumin seeds
  • 1 cup green beans, finely chopped
  • 1 cup carrots, finely chopped
  • 1 cup green peas
  • 2 small green chilies, very finely crushed
  • 4 cups water
  • 2 teaspoons rock salt
  • handful fresh coriander
  • Green Chutney, to serve

Method

  1. Roast the broken wheat porridge lightly in a pan till light brown. Set aside.
  2. In another pan on medium flame, roast the cumin seeds till dark brown. Add the beans, carrots and peas and stir well. Add the crushed chilies and stir again.
  3. Add 4 cups water and bring to a boil. Add the roasted porridge. Cover and cook on medium flame till the porridge absorbs all the water.
  4. Turn off the stove. Add rock salt and keep covered 5 minutes.
  5. Garnish generously with coriander and enjoy with green chutney. Eat within 3–4 hours.

Carrot Raisin Salad

Serves 2 as a main meal

The raisins, tahini and carrots combine into an absolutely yummy dish. Tahini is a paste made from sesame seeds, common in Mediterranean cuisine.

Ingredients

  • 3 cups shredded carrots
  • 1 cup homegrown vegetable sprouts (alfalfa, clover, radish)
  • 2 tablespoon finely chopped mint
  • ¼ cup soaked cashews, chopped
  • 3 tablespoons raisins (kishmish)

Tahini Dressing

  • 1 cup white sesame seeds or 4 tablespoons homemade tahini
  • ½ cup water
  • 4 dates, seedless
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 1 tablespoon powdered jaggery
  • ¼ green chili
  • ½ teaspoon rock salt

Method

Tahini

  1. Toast the sesame seeds in a saucepan over medium heat, stirring constantly, until fragrant and very lightly coloured (not brown), 3–5 minutes. They burn quickly.
  2. Once completely cooled, blend to a smooth paste, about 30 seconds — extra smooth, not gritty.

Salad

  1. Blend 4 tablespoons tahini with water, dates, lemon juice, jaggery, chili and salt until smooth.
  2. Place the carrots, sprouts, mint, cashews and raisins in a large mixing bowl. Pour the dressing on top and serve.
Pre-preparation

Prepare vegetable sprouts.

How to Make Peanut Curd

  1. Soak 1 cup of raw peanuts overnight. Drain & rinse well. Grind with 3 cups water till smooth. Pour into a muslin cloth and squeeze out the peanut milk.
  2. Transfer the peanut milk to a saucepan and bring to a boil on medium heat. It will thicken slightly. Remove from the stove and let it cool down.
  3. To set the curd, take 2 green chilis, remove their crowns and drop them into the milk. Make a small slit in the remaining chilis and drop those in too. Cover and keep aside for 12 hours — it sets into a jelly-like substance. Discard the chili pieces and blend the curd until smooth.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a juice fast?

We drink only fresh juices for 3 days. No solid food is allowed on this fast.

Is it essential for me to do the fast, or can I just attend to learn about juice fasting?

It is essential to do the 3-day fast with us. Only then will you get the maximum benefit from this workshop.

Is it safe?

Yes, it is absolutely safe. People who are pregnant or on very high medication need to make a few adjustments — if you belong in this category, please let us know and we will guide you.

I have health problems / I’m diabetic, can I still fast?

Yes, you can fast. Just ensure you consult your doctor beforehand.

How many juices can I have in a day?

Approximately 4–8 glasses of juice per day.

What if I don’t have access to the vegetables and fruits you mentioned?

You can prepare juices with any available vegetables. It is not important to drink a specific juice — we just want to give the digestive system a break from solid food.

Do I need organic vegetables?

Not necessarily. However, if you can manage to get organic vegetables, that’s the best.

I don’t have a juicer, can I still participate?

Yes. You can do a coconut water fast, or make juices using a blender / mixer — simply add the vegetables and fruits to the blender and then sieve them.

Will I be able to watch the replay of the live webinars later?

All online webinar sessions are recorded and the link is shared with you. However, the replays are only available for 24 hours from the time the session ends. Unless there are network problems, we insist that you watch it live.
